What this is for

The taste-fit layer for anyone who licenses or programs content

The problem

Someone programming a niche channel has to decide which titles to license for a specific audience, and almost no taste data to decide with. The usual substitutes are genre tags, which describe the film rather than the viewer, and a language model, which defaults to what is famous. Fame is a reasonable guess and a poor answer: it is the same guess for every audience.

This puts the decision on an audience's measured affinities instead. The part that matters: it lets the agent decline when the evidence will not carry a decision.

Who it is for

  • Niche SVOD and FAST channel programmers, who acquire against a defined audience rather than a broad one, and whose catalogue decisions repeat every month.
  • Distributors and international sales agents, deciding which titles to put in front of which buyer at a market.
  • Acquisitions teams who already keep a list of what they have licensed. That list is the input; no new data collection is required.

Why now

Channels are proliferating faster than the research budgets that used to support them, and the fallback of asking a general-purpose model produces confident answers with nothing underneath them. An agent making a cultural call needs something to ground it in, and needs to know when it is not grounded.

Why Qloo specifically

The thing no language model holds is the cross-domain signal: that an audience's games and television predict its films. We measured how strongly each domain carries that signal. Television 0.06, video games 0.08 and music 0.11 discriminate sharply; brands, books, podcasts and places barely or not at all. We built only on the three that work. Without the graph there is nothing to resolve titles against, no signatures to cluster, and ranking collapses to whatever the model remembers being famous.

The landing page is that comparison run on a real audience, and the evidence page is every measurement behind it.

Hypothesis, not a price list

We have not sold this. The shape we would test first is a per-seat tier for a programming or acquisitions team, with a catalogue-sized tier above it for groups running several channels, priced against the cost of one wrong licensing decision rather than per query, since the whole point is that the tool sometimes answers "not enough evidence".

Any number here would be invented, so there isn't one.

Where it goes next

  • Other catalogues, same engine. Music programming for a venue or a playlist, and live-event booking, are the same question with a different catalogue. Anchor quality varies by domain and we have only measured it for film, so each would need its own measurement rather than an assumption.
  • Sponsorship and brand fit, where the question is which property suits an audience. Our own measurement puts brands among the weak signals, so that one starts as a negative result to overturn.
  • Catalogue feeds. Today an audience is described by typing titles. Reading an existing acquisitions list directly turns a demo into something used on a Tuesday.
